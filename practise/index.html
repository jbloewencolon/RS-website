Tools for the actual relationship

The principles are elsewhere on this site. This page is where you put them against a relationship you are actually in.

Two are built: a map that separates consent into twenty domains, and a tool for preparing an ending before you need it. Seven more are named but unwritten. Each runs in this browser tab, asks for nothing, and produces no score and no verdict.

⌖ Safety

written relationship exercises are not safe for everyone, and joint exercises are not safe where one person controls the other. This is not always visible from inside a relationship. Each tool opens with a safety check before it starts. Those checks are not a risk assessment or a substitute for specialist support.

⌖ Privacy

everything you type stays in this browser tab. Nothing is sent anywhere, nothing is stored, nothing is counted, and this page is disallowed to search crawlers. The site does not save your answers. Your browser, device, screenshots, downloads, print history, or monitoring software may still leave traces.

Before you bring this up with someone

Use your own words and begin with one concrete issue.

A few things that help:

  • · Bring one thing, not the framework. "I've been thinking about how we decide things" travels better than "I've been reading about relational sovereignty."
  • · Don't hand someone a page and ask them to react. That's a test, and people fail tests defensively.
  • · Say what you want, not what they're doing wrong. The second is an indictment even when it's accurate.
  • · If they decline the exercise, do not use it to press for agreement; decide what you will do with the unresolved issue.

And the one that matters more than the rest: if you're raising this in order to get someone to agree to something they've already declined, this isn't the tool for that, and putting it in this vocabulary will make it worse, not better.

The Consent Domains Map

A relationship label is not blanket permission. This map separates consent into twenty domains so that yes in one is not read as yes in all of them.

Mark where you stand in each domain, add a condition where it matters, then print it or read it aloud with the other person. There is no score and no correct pattern. The map records perceptions at one moment. It does not verify consent, diagnose abuse, or establish a shared account.

Before you open this

This tool asks you to describe a relationship in writing. For most people that is useful. For some people it is not safe, and the difference is not always obvious from inside.

Sit with these for a moment. There is no score and no answer to give anyone. If answering could expose you to monitoring or retaliation, close the page and use a safer device if one is available.

  • · When you disagree, what happens afterwards?
  • · Do you have your own access to money, documents, and a way to leave the house?
  • · Does the other person know where you are most of the time, and did you agree to that?
  • · Have you thought about leaving? If you have said so out loud, what was the response?
  • · Is there anyone who knows the whole of what is happening?
Read these in shorter sentences
  • · After a disagreement, what happens?
  • · Money. ID. A way out. Do you have your own?
  • · Does someone always know where you are? Did you agree to that?
  • · Have you said you might leave? What happened when you did?
  • · Does anyone else know everything that's happening?

If these prompts raise concern about monitoring, isolation, retaliation, or access to essentials, consider leaving this exercise and contacting a specialist service from a safer device. These same five questions are also published on their own, so you can read or send them without opening a tool. If what's difficult is language rather than a question (a phrase like "I'm honouring my sovereignty" used on you, not by you) Learn has a guide to how this vocabulary gets used that way.

Before you start

Choose a time when you have enough privacy, energy, and steadiness for the exercise.

Some people find one of these helps. Some find they make things worse, which is also worth knowing. None of it is required.

  • · Name five things you can see, without moving.
  • · If grounding helps you, push your feet into the floor and notice the floor pushing back.
  • · Hold something with texture (fabric, a cup, a rail) and describe it to yourself in words.
  • · Breathe however you normally breathe. If counting helps you, count. If paying attention to your breathing makes things worse, skip this one.

If none of that appeals, skip it. Going straight to the tool is a legitimate choice. So is closing the tab.

Three care and access domains: Access Intimacy & Body Support

Some care keeps a person alive. Some care keeps a person's paperwork alive. Some care keeps a person company. They are not the same thing, and refusing them does not cost the same thing.

Needing care does not hand anyone authority over you. Providing care does not erase your own needs or limits, whether you are a partner, family member, volunteer, or paid worker. This domain is for both positions, and both of you should fill it in.

After you fill it in

  • · Which domain did you hesitate longest over? That hesitation is the useful part, not the answer.
  • · Where did you assume a yes because of the relationship's name rather than an actual conversation?
  • · What would it cost you to move one domain to no? If the answer is housing, money, or access to children, the consent in that domain is not yet free.
  • · Set a date to look at this again. Consent is ongoing; a map is a snapshot.

An ending, prepared for.

Not a plan for whether to leave. A place to put the logistics down before you need them, and somewhere to put down what isn't logistics at all.

Three parts: what lands where, what's owed on the way out, and after. Only the first one asks you to fill anything in.

If leaving safely means leaving suddenly, close this and go. Nothing on this page is worth staying for.

This tool is for someone with time to prepare, not someone who needs to leave right now. If that's not you, the next click should go to resources, not to this.

Not feelings. Logistics. For each of these, who holds it after you, and do they know they're holding it.

Part two: what you owe on the way out.

What you owe someone at the end isn't a reason. It's:

  • · telling them, rather than making them work it out
  • · not taking back the good parts on the way out, as though loving them had been a mistake you're now correcting
  • · making sure whatever they depended on you for lands somewhere before you go, if you can go safely
  • · letting them grieve at their own speed, which will not be yours

And if going safely means going suddenly, go. Nothing in any of this is a reason to stay.

From the Invitation's "On goodbyes," in full.

Part three: after.

Nothing below asks anything of you. No fields, no prompts, nothing that counts as finished.

Grief for a relationship that's ending, or one that's over, doesn't run on the logic of the rest of this page. It doesn't resolve because the logistics are handled. It can show up long after the audit above is irrelevant, for someone whose name you no longer say out loud.

That's not a problem this tool is built to fix. It isn't a problem. Close the tab whenever you want to. There's nothing left to do here, and there was never going to be.
