Learn — the idea, in plain language · about 17 minutes

Freedom through relationship, not from it.

Relational sovereignty asks what would change if relationships were understood as living systems of consent, responsibility, reciprocity, and self-determination rather than structures of possession.

It is not a finished doctrine, and this page does not present it as one. Thirteen principles follow. Each carries a question you can actually ask of a relationship you are in.

Seven situations, traced through — failures marked as failures

What these principles are like to actually be inside.

Thirteen principles read cleanly in the abstract. Some of them strain, bend, or fail outright for specific people in specific situations — and a framework that only ever illustrates itself with its easy cases is quietly asking readers whose situation is a hard case to notice the gap themselves. Seven aren't illustrations. They're places this framework runs out, named as such rather than smoothed over.

A sponsored partner

One partner's legal status in the country is tied to the other maintaining the sponsorship.

Holds: Principles 01 and 04 still name exactly what's wrong with using that leverage.

Fails: Principle 07, refusal without retaliation. Leaving can mean losing status, family separation, deportation. The retaliation isn't the sponsoring partner's — it's the immigration system's — and nothing here supplies a material floor under that refusal, only Resources to point toward.

A disabled person partnered with their funded attendant

A paid care worker is also an intimate partner — the same hours carry both a wage and a bond.

Holds: Principle 13 names the carer's own position inside the relationship; principle 03 already distinguishes chosen from unchosen dependence.

Fails: Principles 01 and 08 both strain. Funding bodies can require disclosure of the relationship's nature to keep the funding flowing — 08's own carve-out, opacity isn't available against institutions that compel disclosure to release money. Neither person can cleanly refuse one role without risking the other, and this framework has no separate words for "this is paid labour" and "this is chosen intimacy" when they're the same person, the same hours.

A carer of a dependent adult

Ongoing care for someone — an adult child, a parent — who cannot survive without it.

Holds: Principle 13 and the continuity-of-care clause above name the carer's own refusal as real and constrained, not a moral failing.

Fails: Naming a constraint honestly is not the same as relieving it. Principle 07 says refusal should be possible without retaliation; here it's materially bounded by someone else's survival, and nothing on this page supplies the respite, replacement care, or funding that would make refusal actually available. That's a limit of what a website can do, stated rather than hidden.

A person under coercive control

A partner who monitors, isolates, or punishes disagreement — the situation the safety gate on Practise exists for.

Holds: nothing, cleanly — that's the point of naming it separately rather than folding it into the other six.

Fails: nearly every principle at once. 02, 04, and 07 all assume conditions coercive control is specifically built to remove. This isn't a case the principles handle badly — it's the case they were never meant to handle alone, which is the entire reason this framework routes to Resources here instead of offering more principles.

A person under guardianship

A court has formally transferred some or all of someone's decision-making authority to another person.

Holds: principle 08 still distinguishes opacity owed to intimates from disclosure compelled by institutions.

Fails: principle 02, self-determination, by legal design. This framework has no stated position on when that's a justified response to genuine incapacity versus a form of the possession thesis 01 refuses — a tool with a long history of use against disabled, elderly, and disproportionately Indigenous and Black people. Naming the gap here rather than claiming a position it hasn't earned the standing to take.

A precarious worker in shared housing

Housing security depends on staying in good standing with housemates, a landlord, or an employer providing tied housing.

Holds: principle 05 and the manifesto's revised thesis 02 ("whether it can answer for itself") name what's wrong with an arbitrary demand here.

Fails: principle 07 again. Refusing a housemate's demand can mean eviction or homelessness, and a person without secure exit has no practical standing to make anyone answer for themselves, whatever principle 05 says should happen in theory.

A child in a restructuring household

Parents separating, a household reforming, custody changing.

Holds: principles 09 and 12 gesture toward children without addressing them directly.

Fails: no principle here grants a child standing proportionate to what they absorb. The manifesto's revised thesis 11 says the people who absorb a relationship's consequences get to ask — a child absorbs more of a household's restructuring than almost anyone in it, and nothing in these thirteen principles, written for adults capable of exercising refusal and self-determination directly, has an age-appropriate theory of a child's own voice in that process.

⌖ Not exhaustive, and not ranked by severity. Seven situations chosen because each fails a different principle for a different reason — the point isn't the list, it's that a list this short already finds seven of them.

A framework about intimacy that hadn't mentioned sex

Four things named plainly, using the vocabulary already here.

None of these are new concepts for this framework — each one is an existing principle, applied to something the site had otherwise left unsaid. This isn't therapeutic or clinical content, and doesn't claim to be; where it isn't enough, Resources is what's actually built for that.

Desire discrepancy

Wanting sex at a different frequency or intensity than a partner does is not a sign either person is broken, and it isn't automatically solved by whoever wants less doing it anyway. Principle 04 requires consent to be specific and ongoing — a yes given to avoid conflict, guilt, or the relationship ending is not that yes, even when nobody said the word "or else." The same principle protects the higher-desire partner too: wanting sex is not a character flaw to be managed, and going without is not a silent obligation either person owes the other.

Reproductive coercion

Sabotaging contraception, pressuring someone toward or away from pregnancy, or controlling access to abortion or reproductive healthcare is a specific, documented form of intimate partner violence — not a disagreement about family planning. It's coercive control (principles 02 and 04) applied specifically to a body's own reproductive capacity, and it's exactly the situation the safety gate on Practise and the coercive-control listings on Resources already exist for. Named here so it isn't invisible in a framework about intimacy that otherwise doesn't mention it — not because this site has a specialised tool for it beyond what already exists.

Sexual trauma

Past sexual trauma changes what consent negotiation needs to look like. A frozen or compliant response is not the same as a wanted one, and principle 04's requirement that consent be "materially possible" applies to a nervous system's capacity to say no in the moment, not only to whether someone had the legal standing to. This site is not a trauma-therapy resource and doesn't pretend to be one — see Resources for what is. What this framework can say: checking in rather than assuming, treating withdrawal as information rather than rejection, and expecting inconsistency rather than reading it as dishonesty is part of what principle 04 already asks for, not an addition to it.

Consensual power exchange

Deliberately structured power difference — D/s dynamics, protocol, negotiated control — is not what principle 05 refuses. Principle 05's own test is whether power is arbitrary, hidden, or impossible to challenge, not whether power exists at all. Named, bounded, revocable power exchange, agreed to specifically and open to renegotiation, is closer to what principle 05 is actually asking for than a relationship that claims to have no power dynamics and therefore never examines the ones it has. What isn't consensual power exchange: anything that can't be renegotiated, refused, or ended without retaliation — at that point it's principle 07 doing the work, and principle 05 no longer applies.

A clause, not a principle — 06 and 07 without a tiebreaker

Continuity of care

Leaving is not abandonment. But when someone depends on you for survival — food, medication, housing, personal care, money, immigration status — your leaving moves that need somewhere else. It does not dissolve it, and the person holding it next is usually already tired.

So if you can go safely: before you go, name who now holds what you were holding, and check that they know they are holding it. If nobody does, say that out loud to someone who is not the person you are leaving.

If going safely means going suddenly, go suddenly. Nothing in this framework is a reason to stay. This is written against people who use the word sovereignty to walk away from a dependant — and it is not available to anyone who wants to use it to keep you.

Who is holding what I was holding, and do they know it?

Principle 06 (relational accountability) and principle 07 (refusal) can pull against each other with nothing here to say which wins. This clause is the tiebreaker for one specific case — dependency, at the moment of leaving — not a general answer. If you are not sure whether leaving is safe, Resources has somewhere to start.

Which principle wins — answered where it can be, left open where it can't

Two adjudication questions, two different answers.

06 (accountability) vs. 07 (refusal): no rule, deliberately.

Beyond the dependency case above, this framework does not rank these against each other, and that's a position, not an oversight. Principle 07 protects the refusal itself — the grief, disruption, or upheaval that follows someone saying no is not evidence that principle 06 was violated. What 06 actually governs is the manner of an ending where real obligations exist — a lease, a joint decision, a plan someone else built their own choices around — not whether the ending is allowed. Past that distinction, how much notice is owed, how much disruption is acceptable, and what counts as reasonable care in a given ending depends on facts a general principle can't specify in advance. A formula precise enough to resolve every case would be false precision dressed as an answer.

08 (opacity) vs. thesis 11 (no private empires): this one has an answer.

Principle 08 already narrows itself: opacity is a right against people who want more of you than they're owed, not against institutions that compel disclosure to release care, money, status, or documents. The manifesto's thesis 11 narrows the other side — standing to demand an account belongs to the people who absorb a relationship's consequences, not the state, not a crowd with an opinion. Put together: opacity holds fully against anyone without standing, and even against someone who has it, what's owed is the specific thing they'll absorb — not a full accounting of the relationship. That's the whole reasoning behind the endings tool on Practise asking only "who holds this after, and do they know," never "explain the relationship."

⌖ The Repair Protocol (in progress, see the roadmap) answers a different question — what's the process after harm — not which principle governs a standing conflict between two of them. Both questions were open; only one had a process already planned to answer it.

One word, four jobs

Four things this word is doing.

Political sovereignty.

Jurisdiction over territory and people. Indigenous nationhood. This site does not touch it and cannot advance it.

Jurisdictional sovereignty.

Whose law governs a body of data, on whose territory the machine sits, who holds deletion authority. This is the sense in which a website can have anything to do with sovereignty at all.

Relational sovereignty.

Authority over the terms of one's own relations — the sense borrowed from Matthew Wildcat and, independently, from work on assistive technology. This is the site's operative sense.

The rhetorical sense.

Not being owned. This is the manifesto's register and it is the loosest of the four.

The first and the third are not the same kind of thing. Where this site is persuasive partly because they rhyme, that is worth naming rather than using.

And an objection this site has not answered

Taiaiake Alfred argues sovereignty is inseparable from the frameworks of colonisation, and that Indigenous governance is better served by concepts that are not borrowed from the people who used this one to negate it. That argument is on the shelf. This site keeps the word because Wildcat rebuilt it and because the jurisdictional question — whose law governs here, who holds deletion authority — is the right question for intimacy and "autonomy" cannot ask it. That is a reason, not a refutation. If the paid review says the word does more harm than work, the word goes and this paragraph becomes the record of why it stayed this long.

The word this framework needs and doesn't have yet

Two sovereigns, one shared condition. Sovereignty doesn't have a form for that. Treaty does.

"Relational sovereignty" (the third sense above) names a value — authority over the terms of one's own relations — without naming the form that value takes when two people hold it at once, in the same relationship, neither one's authority cancelling the other's. Political theory already built that form. It's called treaty.

The Two Row Wampum, used elsewhere on this site as a layout figure — two vessels on one river, neither steering the other, a stretch of water belonging to both — is not a metaphor invented for that purpose. It's a specific treaty relationship, between specific nations, with its own terms, its own history of being violated, and its own ongoing life independent of anything this site does with it. Naming it as an influence on how this framework thinks about two-party non-domination is not the same claim as saying a couple, a household, or a friendship is a treaty relationship. It isn't. The stakes, the histories, and the legal weight are not equivalent, and treating them as equivalent would be exactly the kind of "lifestyle experimentation" borrowing of Indigenous political thought that this site's own stated limits already rule out.

What's actually borrowed is narrower and more useful: a treaty isn't a one-time agreement, it's a standing practice of renewal — terms that get revisited, not terms that get set once and assumed to hold. This framework asserts non-domination (principle 05) as a value but has, so far, built no equivalent practice of its own. The closest gesture on the site is "The Relational Review," still on the not-yet-built list — a seasonal check-in on what feels alive, what feels assumed, what needs revision. That's the treaty logic, unbuilt. Two sovereigns sharing a condition don't stay in right relation by having agreed once. They stay in right relation by renewing.

⌖ This is named as the framework's central unsolved problem, not a solved one. Sovereignty supplies the value; treaty supplies the shape a working answer would need to have. Nothing on this site yet does what a treaty does — set terms, revisit them, hold both parties to the revisiting.

A field, not a ladder

Forms and labels, none of them the default.

Relationship anarchy names the refusal directly: no automatic ranking of romantic partnership above friendship, chosen kin, or care — Andie Nordgren's manifesto is the clearest short statement of it. Amatonormativity names what's being refused: the assumption, built into law, custom, and most relationship advice, that exclusive romantic couplehood is universal and owed the top of the stack — the term is Elizabeth Brake's. Neither word is a lifestyle prescription. Both are a description of a default this framework declines to treat as neutral.

A field, not a ladder, means the labels below sit at the same height. This list is alphabetical, not a ranking by seriousness, permanence, or virtue — nothing here outranks anything else, and the framework does not treat any one of them as the form the others are measured against.

  • Asexual and aromantic partnership
  • Chosen family
  • Co-parenting without romance
  • Kitchen-table and parallel polyamory
  • Long-distance and non-cohabiting partnership
  • Monogamy
  • Platonic life partnership
  • Polyamory
  • Queerplatonic partnership
  • Relationship anarchy
  • Solo polyamory

Not exhaustive — a person's own word for what they have is the one that governs, including one not listed here.

⌖ A field is not a floor. Naming these forms as equals doesn't make the risk of choosing one equal to the risk of choosing another — legal recognition, immigration status, custody, and inheritance still fall unevenly across them, unrelated to which one serves the people in it best. See Resources for where that risk gets concrete.

This site's own vocabulary, turned around

How this vocabulary gets used as a weapon.

Every word on this site can be turned around and used on someone. That isn't a reason not to write them down. It's a reason to write down how it happens.

Ten phrases below, most of them phrases this site's own vocabulary makes available, each with the diagnostic question worth asking when you hear it — from yourself or from someone else.

One page or two, depending on your browser and paper size — just this list, not the rest of Learn.

"I just want you to become your best self."

Sounds like care. Often is care. Also the standard vocabulary of someone renovating a person. If somebody else is holding the blueprint for who you're supposed to become, that isn't growth.

Whose picture of your best self is this, and were you asked?

"That's my boundary."

A boundary is something you do — what you will and won't be part of. It isn't an instruction for someone else's behaviour, and it isn't a way to close a conversation about something you did.

Does this boundary govern them, or you?

"I don't have the capacity for this."

True constantly, for everyone. Also a way to be permanently unavailable for accountability while remaining available for everything else.

Is there any version of later?

"You're being anxious." "That's avoidant." "That's textbook narcissism."

Clinical words in a domestic argument aren't analysis. They turn a disagreement into a symptom, so it doesn't have to be answered.

Could this sentence be said without the diagnosis?

"You're being possessive." "That's so amatonormative."

Sometimes true. Also the fastest way to make a real objection sound like a character flaw. Monogamy is a legitimate choice. Wanting to be told things is not a pathology.

Is the objection being answered, or reclassified?

"I'm honouring my sovereignty."

This site's own word. It has never meant leaving without telling anyone, and it has never meant walking away from someone who depends on you to survive.

What's being honoured, and what's being avoided?

"We need to work on this." "We've decided."

The "we" is doing work here — check what it's covering for.

Swap "we" for "you." Is the sentence still true?

"Hold it more spaciously." "You're being so transactional."

Money, time, labour, and safety aren't the opposite of love. They're what love runs on. A frame in which material questions are spiritually crude is a frame that benefits whoever has more.

Is the concrete question still on the table?

"Everyone agrees." "The pod thinks."

A collective voice is easy to invoke and hard to check.

Has anyone said this to you individually?

"You seemed fine with it."

Seeming fine is what people do when the alternative is expensive.

What would it have cost to not seem fine?

⌖ What got said to you, in this vocabulary, to avoid answering something — that's the strongest candidate this site has for something a reader could add. There's nowhere built to route it yet. It's named on Contribute, parked, not built, until there's a community to route it to.

Take it further

Read the manifesto for the refusal these principles come from. Work through the Consent Domains Map to apply them. Then leave for the writers themselves — this page is a summary, and a summary is not the thought.

A caution held throughout

The relational is not virtuous simply by being relational. Kinship, community, and tradition contain their own hierarchies, and a design that signals warmth while leaving power unmapped has produced decoration, not sovereignty.
