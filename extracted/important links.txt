

No single source contains the entire theory developed in the manifesto. The closest foundations are Wildcat on relational sovereignty, Kuokkanen on relational freedom, TallBear on caretaking relations, feminist relational-autonomy scholarship, disability-justice writing on interdependence, and transformative-justice work on accountability.

The list also preserves an essential caution from the aesthetic research: Indigenous relationality, sovereignty, refusal, and survivance should not be converted into a generic radical style. Indigenous traditions are nation-specific, and punk appropriation can reproduce colonial extraction even when it presents itself as rebellion.

Items marked ★ are the strongest starting points.

1. Relational sovereignty and Indigenous relational freedom

★ Matthew Wildcat, “Replacing Exclusive Sovereignty with a Relational Sovereignty”The most direct foundation for the term. Wildcat contrasts sovereignty organized through exclusion and separation with sovereignty built by weaving together relationships, authorities, jurisdictions, and Indigenous political orders. Article, open access. (Paradigm)

★ Rauna Kuokkanen, “It’s About All Relations: Indigenous Feminist Theory of Relational Freedom”Probably the most important single article for the manifesto. Kuokkanen joins relationality to freedom as non-domination, while warning that family, community, tradition, and Indigenous governance can contain internal gendered and other inequalities. Peer-reviewed article, open access. (Cambridge University Press & Assessment)

Matt Wildcat and Daniel Voth, “Indigenous Relationality: Definitions and Methods”Distinguishes global Indigenous relationality, nation-specific relational systems, and inter-Indigenous relations. This is essential for avoiding vague or pan-Indigenous uses of “relationality.” Peer-reviewed article. (Sage Journals)

Rauna Kuokkanen, “Restructuring Relations: Indigenous Self-Determination, Governance, and Gender”Examines why political self-determination is incomplete when gendered power, violence, resource access, and participation inside Indigenous societies are ignored. Book. (Rauna Kuokkanen)

JiWoong Jang, Patrick Carrington, and Andrew Begel, “From Autonomy to Sovereignty: A New Telos for Socially Assistive Technology”A recent and unusually direct use of “relational sovereignty.” It defines sovereignty as the power to choose between independence and interdependence, shifting the question from “Can someone do it alone?” to “Do they get to decide?” Research paper, open access. 2. Decolonization, possession, land, kinship, and intimacy

★ Kim TallBear, “Caretaking Relations, Not American Dreaming”Proposes caretaking relations among human and more-than-human beings as an alternative to settler narratives of progress, hierarchy, possession, and exceptionalism. It strongly supports the manifesto’s emphasis on present obligations rather than abstract declarations of love or freedom. Article. (Kim TallBear)

★ Kim TallBear, “Identity Is a Poor Substitute for Relating: Genetic Ancestry, Critical Polyamory, Property, and Relations”Connects Indigenous critiques of identity and property with critical polyamory and the active practice of maintaining relations. Book chapter. (Kim TallBear)

Kim TallBear, “The Polyamorist That Wants to Destroy Sex”An accessible interview on monogamy, marriage, settler sexuality, Indigenous relationality, and why decolonizing intimacy requires more than adding additional romantic partners. Interview. (Critical Polyamorist)

★ Leanne Betasamosake Simpson, “As We Have Always Done: Indigenous Freedom through Radical Resistance”Grounds Indigenous resurgence in land-based practices, embodied freedom, refusal, queer and gendered relations, community, and the rejection of settler colonialism. Book. (University of Minnesota Press)

Aileen Moreton-Robinson, “The White Possessive: Property, Power, and Indigenous Sovereignty”Provides the larger political theory behind the manifesto’s critique of possession. It examines how settler sovereignty operates through a possessive logic involving land, bodies, institutions, knowledge, and national identity. Book. (University of Minnesota Press)

★ Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang, “Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor”A necessary limit on the manifesto’s language. Decolonization cannot simply mean improving communication, rejecting social conventions, or developing healthier relationships. It centrally concerns Indigenous land and life. Peer-reviewed article, open access. (Journal Production Services)

Audra Simpson, “Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States”Develops a politics of refusal, sovereignty, membership, and self-representation through Kahnawà:ke Mohawk political life. It supports the manifesto’s distinction between recognition granted by dominant powers and sovereignty enacted through refusal. Book. (Duke University Press)

Audra Simpson, “Consent’s Revenge”Challenges contractual and colonial fantasies in which consent is abstracted from history, coercion, dispossession, and power. Particularly useful for developing the manifesto’s claim that formal agreement alone does not make a relationship just. Article, open access. (HYPERLINK "https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.14506/ca31.3.02?utm_source=chatgpt.com" \o "CONSENT'S REVENGE - SIMPSON - 2016 - Cultural Anthropology - Wiley Online Library"AnthroSource)

3. Relation, opacity, refusal, and survivance

★ Édouard Glissant, “Poetics of Relation”A foundational account of identity formed through relation rather than isolation. Glissant’s “right to opacity” supports the principle that intimacy and recognition do not require making another person completely transparent or legible. Book. (University of Michigan Press)

David Garneau, “Imaginary Spaces of Conciliation and Reconciliation: Art, Curation, and Healing”Develops the importance of Indigenous spaces that are not organized for settler access, interpretation, reconciliation, or consumption. It supports the manifesto’s defence of protocol, withholding, privacy, and culturally governed knowledge. Book chapter. (De Gruyter Brill)

Jolene Rickard, “Visualizing Sovereignty in the Time of Biometric Sensors”Treats Indigenous visual production as an exercise of sovereignty and political continuity rather than simply cultural representation. It helps ground the idea that sovereignty includes control over the conditions under which one is seen and interpreted. Peer-reviewed article. (EBSCO OpenURL)

Gerald Vizenor, “Manifest Manners: Narratives on Postindian Survivance”Introduces survivance as active Indigenous presence and continuance rather than victimhood, disappearance, or mere survival. This supports the manifesto’s rejection of punk’s “no future” nihilism in favour of regeneration and futurity. Book. (University of Nebraska Press)

4. Relational autonomy, interdependence, and care

★ Catriona Mackenzie and Natalie Stoljar, eds., “Relational Autonomy: Feminist Perspectives on Autonomy, Agency, and the Social Self”The foundational philosophical collection on how autonomy is socially formed, supported, or impaired. It directly supports the claim that autonomy does not require isolation or self-sufficiency. Edited academic collection. (OUP Academic)

“Feminist Perspectives on Autonomy,” Stanford Encyclopedia of PhilosophyThe best accessible overview of relational autonomy, oppressive socialization, adaptive preferences, self-trust, agency, and the social conditions needed for meaningful choice. Reference article, free. (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

Joan Tronto, “Caring Democracy: Markets, Equality, and Justice”Argues that care is a public and political responsibility, not merely private feminine labour. It supports the manifesto’s demand to make care visible, distribute it fairly, and connect freedom to collective responsibility. Book. (NYU Press)

Eva Feder Kittay, “Love’s Labor: Essays on Women, Equality and Dependency”Examines dependency, equality, disability, caregiving, and the injustice created when care labour is treated as natural, private, or economically insignificant. Book. (Routledge)

★ Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, “Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice”A practical and political account of care webs, collective access, emotional labour, mutual aid, disability justice, and communities built beyond compulsory independence. Essay collection. (Arsenal Pulp Press)

Sins Invalid, “10 Principles of Disability Justice”A concise framework centred on intersectionality, leadership by those most affected, anti-capitalism, recognition of wholeness, interdependence, collective access, and collective liberation. Movement statement, free. (Sins Invalid)

Mia Mingus, “Access Intimacy: The Missing Link”Describes intimacy created when someone understands and responds to another person’s access needs without making that support humiliating, resentful, charitable, or a source of ownership. Blog essay, free. (Leaving Evidence)

5. Relationship hierarchy, custom commitments, and possession

★ Andie Nordgren, “The Short Instructional Manifesto for Relationship Anarchy”The primary relationship-anarchy text. It introduces abundant love, customized commitments, trust, autonomy, rejection of entitlement, and resistance to automatically ranking romantic relationships over other relationships. Manifesto, free. (Andie's Log)

Elizabeth Brake, “Minimizing Marriage: Marriage, Morality, and the Law”Develops the concept of amatonormativity, the social assumption that exclusive romantic couplehood should be universal and superior to friendship, chosen kinship, collective care, and other adult relationships. Book. (OUP Academic)

Evan Stark, “Coercive Control: How Men Entrap Women in Personal Life”Demonstrates that domination within relationships often operates through surveillance, isolation, economic restrictions, intimidation, and control of ordinary life, not only through individual incidents of physical violence. Book. (OUP Academic)

Karen Levy and Bruce Schneier, “Privacy Threats in Intimate Relationships”Shows why privacy remains necessary within families, friendships, caregiving relationships, and romantic partnerships. Intimacy creates distinctive risks because close others possess physical access, personal knowledge, trust, and potential coercive power. Peer-reviewed article, open access. (OUP Academic)

6. Accountability, repair, and transformative justice

★ Creative Interventions, “Toolkit: A Practical Guide to Stop Interpersonal Violence”One of the most extensive practical resources for community accountability and non-carceral responses to interpersonal harm. It includes processes, worksheets, stories, safety planning, accountability practices, and approaches to long-term transformation. Toolkit, free. (creative interventions)

Mia Mingus, “The Four Parts of Accountability and How to Give a Genuine Apology”A clear and practical account of self-reflection, apology, repair, and changed behaviour. It directly supports the manifesto’s claim that apology without material or behavioural change is not repair. Blog essay, free. (Leaving Evidence)

Ching-In Chen, Jai Dulani, and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, eds., “The Revolution Starts at Home: Confronting Intimate Violence Within Activist Communities”Addresses the contradiction between public commitments to justice and private violence within activist communities. It provides survivor-centred approaches to community accountability and challenges the idea that radical politics automatically produces ethical relationships. Edited collection. (Google Books)

Ejeris Dixon and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, eds., “Beyond Survival: Strategies and Stories from the Transformative Justice Movement”Collects practical models for responding to violence without relying exclusively on punishment, policing, incarceration, or social disposal. Edited collection. (Google Books)

Suggested reading sequence

For the fastest route into the framework, read these in order:

Wildcat, “Replacing Exclusive Sovereignty”

Kuokkanen, “It’s About All Relations”

TallBear, “Caretaking Relations, Not American Dreaming”

Tuck and Yang, “Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor”

Mackenzie and Stoljar, Relational Autonomy

Mingus, “Access Intimacy”

Nordgren, Relationship Anarchy Manifesto

Brake, Minimizing Marriage

Stark, Coercive Control

Creative Interventions, Toolkit

Together, these provide the strongest foundation for relational sovereignty as a synthesis of self-determination, non-domination, chosen interdependence, anti-possession, consent, opacity, material freedom, differentiated responsibility, care, refusal, and accountable repair.
