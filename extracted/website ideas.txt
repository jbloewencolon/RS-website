

A strong website should feel less like a relationship-advice platform and more like a living commons, archive, zine library, workshop, and practice space. It should invite people to question inherited relationship structures, while giving them concrete tools for building accountable alternatives.

The visual identity can draw from punk’s DIY, photocopied, anti-institutional language, but it should not treat Indigenous imagery as a generic symbol of resistance. The supplied research explicitly distinguishes punk and anarchist aesthetics from sovereign Indigenous traditions, which remain nation-specific and governed by distinct protocols.

Relational Sovereignty Commons

Concept for a Website, Archive, and Practical Learning Platform

1. Core Purpose

The website should help people:

understand relational sovereignty;

recognize colonial, possessive, extractive, and hierarchical patterns in relationships;

imagine alternatives to ownership, compulsory roles, and isolated individualism;

practise consent, interdependence, accountability, refusal, and repair;

build healthier romantic, friendship, kinship, community, and ecological relationships;

encounter different intellectual, cultural, activist, and artistic traditions related to the concept;

contribute their own experiences without being pressured into total disclosure;

and develop relationships that support both freedom and responsibility.

The platform should not present relational sovereignty as a finished doctrine.

It should function as an invitation:

What would change if relationships were understood as living systems of consent, responsibility, reciprocity, and self-determination rather than structures of possession?

2. Foundational Position

The website should begin with several clear statements.

Relational sovereignty is not radical individualism

It does not mean:

doing whatever one wants;

refusing all obligations;

avoiding commitment;

treating other people as interchangeable;

prioritizing personal freedom over collective consequences;

or using therapeutic language to escape accountability.

Relational sovereignty recognizes that people are interdependent.

Freedom exists through relationships, resources, care, community, and material support.

Relational sovereignty is not synonymous with relationship anarchy

Relationship anarchy is an important influence, particularly in its critique of compulsory hierarchy, romantic supremacy, entitlement, and predetermined relationship roles.

Relational sovereignty places additional emphasis on:

material dependency;

collective and community responsibilities;

power differences;

caregiving;

children and dependants;

land and more-than-human relationships;

culturally specific protocols;

and responsibilities created through shared histories.

Decolonizing relationships is not a substitute for decolonization

The website must explicitly state that interpersonal transformation does not replace Indigenous jurisdiction, land return, political self-determination, cultural resurgence, or material restitution.

“Decolonizing relationships” should be used carefully to examine how colonial logics appear within intimacy, family, sexuality, gender, property, care, and social institutions.

It must not reduce decolonization to:

better communication;

personal healing;

alternative dating;

lifestyle experimentation;

or individual self-expression.

Indigenous relationality is not one universal philosophy

The platform should acknowledge that Indigenous nations possess distinct laws, teachings, kinship systems, territories, languages, and protocols.

It should resist presenting concepts such as relationality, reciprocity, land relationship, survivance, or sovereignty as interchangeable spiritual principles.

Indigenous sources should be presented through their authors, nations, contexts, and specific intellectual traditions.

3. The Central Website Experience

The site should combine four experiences.

A learning archive

A curated collection of books, articles, interviews, artworks, podcasts, videos, zines, legal texts, and community resources.

A practice workshop

Interactive exercises and printable tools that help visitors examine their own relationships.

A public commons

A carefully moderated space for stories, questions, reflections, creative contributions, and collective knowledge.

A punk publication

A living digital zine that publishes manifestos, interventions, illustrations, provocations, interviews, and experimental writing.

The website should feel alive, unfinished, and participatory.

It should not resemble a corporate wellness platform.

5. Core Principles to Organize the Site

The entire website could be organized around twelve principles.

5.1 No ownership

People do not become property through romance, marriage, sexuality, family, caregiving, or shared history.

Practical question

What access am I treating as an entitlement?

5.2 Self-determination

Each person should retain meaningful authority over their body, identity, beliefs, associations, and life direction.

Practical question

Which decisions belong primarily to me, and which decisions genuinely require shared governance?

5.3 Chosen interdependence

Needing people does not eliminate sovereignty.

Practical question

Do I have meaningful influence over the conditions under which I receive or provide care?

5.4 Consent

Consent must be specific, informed, ongoing, and materially possible.

Practical question

What would happen if someone said no?

5.5 Non-domination

Power should not be arbitrary, hidden, or impossible to challenge.

Practical question

Who controls the resources, information, recognition, or access in this relationship?

5.6 Relational accountability

Freedom does not remove responsibility for foreseeable effects on others.

Practical question

Who bears the cost of my choices?

5.7 Refusal

A relationship should contain space for disagreement, boundaries, withdrawal, and change.

Practical question

Can refusal occur without retaliation?

5.8 Opacity

Intimacy does not require complete access or total legibility.

Practical question

What parts of another person am I assuming I have a right to know?

5.9 Reciprocity

Relationships involve giving and receiving, but reciprocity does not require identical contributions.

Practical question

Are care, effort, risk, and benefit distributed in a way people experience as fair?

5.10 Repair

Accountability requires more than apology.

Practical question

What material or behavioural change would repair require?

5.11 Transformation

Relationships may change form without becoming meaningless.

Practical question

Are we preserving the relationship, or merely preserving its current category?

5.12 Relational futurity

The purpose is not simply to reject harmful relationships, but to create forms of connection in which people and communities can flourish.

Practical question

What kind of relational world are our present practices creating?

6. Practical Tools

6.1 The Relationship Constellation Map

Visitors create a visual map of the people, communities, places, animals, institutions, and living systems with which they are connected.

Instead of placing a romantic partner automatically at the centre, the visitor maps:

emotional support;

practical care;

creative partnership;

intellectual connection;

sexuality;

family;

cultural belonging;

emergency support;

spirituality;

land;

and community.

The tool should ask:

Which relationships receive most of your time?

Which relationships perform invisible care?

Which relationships are important but socially unrecognized?

Who depends on you?

On whom do you depend?

Which relationships have become neglected?

Which relationships are expected to meet too many needs?

6.2 The Power and Dependency Audit

This tool helps people examine power without assuming that all relationships are equal.

Categories could include:

income;

housing;

transportation;

citizenship;

healthcare;

childcare;

social status;

education;

physical ability;

technology access;

community belonging;

professional authority;

emotional support;

and ability to leave.

The tool should not produce a simplistic score.

It should generate discussion questions:

Where is power concentrated?

Is that power visible?

Is it limited?

Can it be challenged?

What safeguards exist?

What would happen during conflict?

What resources would allow each person to make meaningful choices?

6.3 The Consent Domains Map

This exercise separates consent into different domains.

Visitors can review:

physical touch;

sexuality;

contraception;

sexual health;

emotional disclosure;

public identification;

social media;

photographs;

location sharing;

passwords;

money;

shared property;

caregiving;

family involvement;

contact with former partners;

cultural knowledge;

and participation in community events.

This prevents a general relationship label from becoming blanket permission.

6.4 The Personal, Consultative, Joint, and Collective Decisions Tool

Visitors place decisions into four categories.

Personal

One person possesses final authority.

Examples:

bodily consent;

personal identity;

private beliefs;

whether to remain in a relationship.

Consultative

One person retains final authority, but others are meaningfully affected.

Examples:

changing jobs;

moving;

altering availability;

beginning another relationship.

Joint

The decision involves shared responsibilities or resources.

Examples:

shared debt;

co-parenting;

housing;

sexual-health agreements;

shared property.

Collective

The decision affects a wider network or community.

Examples:

community resources;

cultural materials;

dependants;

land;

collaborative institutions.

This tool can reveal when someone is treating a shared decision as personal or a personal decision as though another person owns it.

6.5 The Care Labour Inventory

Participants identify:

domestic labour;

emotional labour;

scheduling;

conflict mediation;

financial administration;

transportation;

childcare;

elder care;

disability support;

community labour;

memory work;

and social maintenance.

The tool should ask:

Who notices the task?

Who initiates it?

Who completes it?

Who is thanked?

Who sacrifices time?

Who has authority over the outcome?

6.6 The Boundary and Refusal Builder

This tool helps visitors formulate boundaries without confusing them with commands.

Template:

When ______ happens, I experience ______.I am willing to participate under these conditions: ______.I am not willing to participate in: ______.If this continues, I will: ______.

The tool should distinguish:

request;

agreement;

boundary;

ultimatum;

threat;

and coercion.

6.7 The Relational Review

A monthly, seasonal, or annual check-in could ask:

What currently feels alive?

What feels assumed?

What has changed?

Where is resentment accumulating?

Where is care uneven?

What commitments remain meaningful?

Which commitments need revision?

What has not been said?

Where is more autonomy needed?

Where is more support needed?

Is repair required?

Should the relationship continue, deepen, transform, pause, or end?

6.8 The Repair Map

The tool should guide participants through:

What happened?

Who was affected?

What conditions made the harm possible?

What responsibility does each participant hold?

What needs to stop immediately?

What would repair require?

What restitution is possible?

What safeguards must change?

Who should participate in the process?

Does the harmed person want continued relationship?

It should explicitly state that repair does not guarantee reconciliation.

6.9 The Exit and Transition Plan

Relational sovereignty must address endings.

The tool could cover:

safety;

communication;

housing;

finances;

shared belongings;

children;

animals;

digital access;

community relationships;

confidentiality;

caregiving;

and future contact.

A responsible ending should not require continued intimacy, but it may require fair treatment of shared responsibilities.

Practical exercises

Ask each person to complete:

A romantic partner does not automatically have the right to ______.

Commitment means ______.

Exclusivity means ______.

Privacy means ______.

Betrayal means ______.

Care means ______.

Freedom means ______.

Our shared responsibilities include ______.

Jealousy without ownership

Visitors examine whether jealousy is connected to:

fear of abandonment;

loss of status;

comparison;

unmet needs;

secrecy;

broken agreements;

economic insecurity;

or beliefs about possession.

The site should not treat jealousy as either proof of love or evidence of personal failure.

Relationship agreement builder

The agreement should cover:

time;

sex;

affection;

privacy;

money;

health;

other relationships;

conflict;

care;

public presentation;

and review dates.

The agreement should be understood as revisable rather than permanent.

8. Friendship

The friendship pathway should challenge the idea that friendship is informal, disposable, or secondary.

Key topics

friendship commitment;

care during crisis;

maintaining connection;

unequal availability;

romantic displacement;

jealousy between friends;

conflict;

chosen family;

and friendship endings.

Practical tools

Friendship commitment conversation

Questions could include:

What does this friendship mean to us?

How do we show care?

What kinds of support can we offer?

What support can we not offer?

How do we handle long silences?

What happens when romantic relationships change our availability?

How do we address hurt?

What would a meaningful long-term friendship look like?

The friendship maintenance plan

Visitors could create realistic commitments involving:

communication frequency;

recurring gatherings;

emergency support;

celebration;

childcare;

creative collaboration;

and shared rituals.

The goal is not to bureaucratize friendship.

It is to make friendship visible enough to protect it from constant displacement.

9. Family and Kinship

This pathway should distinguish care from entitlement.

Key topics

biological and chosen kinship;

parental authority;

adult children;

family obligation;

cultural continuity;

caregiving;

inheritance;

family secrets;

estrangement;

intergenerational trauma;

and boundaries.

Practical exercises

Inherited role inventory

Visitors identify messages such as:

The eldest child must ______.

Mothers should ______.

Fathers should ______.

Children owe parents ______.

Family matters must never ______.

A good sibling always ______.

Leaving the family means ______.

They then evaluate:

Who benefits from this rule?

Who carries its burden?

Was it consciously chosen?

Does it protect care or protect control?

Should it be retained, modified, or refused?

Kinship beyond biology

The platform could help visitors recognize:

chosen family;

ceremonial kin;

co-parents;

queer kinship;

mentors;

neighbours;

community elders;

and long-term care relationships.

It should avoid assuming that chosen family is always safe or that biological family is always oppressive.

All relationships remain subject to accountability.

10. Children and Parenting

The parenting pathway should frame children as developing persons rather than parental possessions.

Key topics

bodily autonomy;

age-appropriate consent;

privacy;

participation in decisions;

discipline;

emotional regulation;

digital surveillance;

family separation;

and increasing autonomy.

Practical exercises

The child participation ladder

For each decision, parents consider whether the child should:

be informed;

be consulted;

have meaningful choices;

participate in the decision;

or hold primary authority.

The answer changes according to age, maturity, safety, and the nature of the decision.

Replace obedience with capacity

Instead of asking only, “How do I make the child comply?” the parent asks:

What skill is missing?

What need is being expressed?

What environmental condition is contributing?

What limit is genuinely necessary?

Can the child understand the reason?

Is the response proportionate?

Is authority being used for protection or convenience?

11. Caregiving and Disability

This pathway should treat care as a relationship involving the sovereignty of both the recipient and caregiver.

Key topics

access;

dependency;

decision-making;

dignity of risk;

caregiver limits;

burnout;

medical authority;

support technologies;

and collective care.

Practical exercises

Care preference profile

The person receiving care identifies:

what support they want;

how it should be offered;

what forms feel intrusive;

what privacy they need;

how decisions should be made;

and what should happen during emergencies.

Caregiver sustainability plan

The caregiver identifies:

limits;

respite needs;

available support;

tasks they can perform;

tasks they cannot perform;

and where additional collective or institutional care is required.

The site should reject the idea that love requires unlimited caregiving capacity.

12. Relationships With Land and the Natural World

This section should not treat nature as a wellness product.

It should move visitors from appreciation to relationship, and from relationship to obligation.

Key topics

place;

territory;

watershed;

food systems;

local species;

environmental responsibility;

land dispossession;

Indigenous jurisdiction;

climate change;

and more-than-human relations.

Practical pathway

Learn where you are

Visitors identify:

the territory on which they live;

the Indigenous nations connected to that territory;

the watershed;

local ecosystems;

significant species;

environmental threats;

and local governance structures.

Move beyond land acknowledgement

The site should ask:

What responsibilities follow from knowing where you are?

Which Indigenous-led organizations can be supported?

What land or water defence efforts exist?

What resources do you consume?

What pollution or waste do you produce?

What relationships with local species can be restored?

What political action is required?

Create a reciprocity practice

Visitors select recurring actions such as:

watershed restoration;

native planting;

habitat protection;

food cultivation;

reducing harmful consumption;

supporting Indigenous land initiatives;

learning local ecological histories;

participating in community stewardship;

or contributing money and labour.

The practice should be specific, ongoing, and accountable.

It should not involve inventing ceremonies or appropriating Indigenous practices.

14. Opacity as a Design Feature

Most archives assume that more access is always better.

A relational sovereignty archive should recognize that ethical archives also require limits.

The site could contain:

public materials;

contributor-controlled materials;

community-specific materials;

temporarily restricted materials;

excerpts without full documents;

metadata-only records;

and records noting that something exists but is not available for public access.

This is particularly important for Indigenous and culturally sensitive materials.

The design should communicate:

Absence does not mean the archive is incomplete. Sometimes withholding is part of ethical preservation.

This principle is especially significant because punk’s tendency toward total exposure can conflict with Indigenous practices of protocol, opacity, and refusal. The supplied research identifies this tension directly.

15. Visual Identity

Punk influence

The site can use:

cut-and-paste typography;

photocopied textures;

scanned handwriting;

annotations;

marginal notes;

stamps;

torn-paper divisions;

zine layouts;

black-and-white imagery;

layered posters;

and visible revision marks.

The site should feel self-published rather than institutionally polished.

Avoiding visual appropriation

The design should not use:

generic “tribal” patterns;

Indigenous sacred symbols;

pan-Indigenous motifs;

feathers, medicine wheels, petroglyphs, or beadwork as decorative shorthand;

or images detached from nation, artist, and protocol.

Where Indigenous artwork appears, it should be:

commissioned or licensed;

attributed;

contextualized;

governed by the artist’s terms;

and presented as a sovereign work rather than visual atmosphere.

Design tension

The site could deliberately combine two visual modes.

The public zine layer

Loud, confrontational, reproducible, accessible, and outward-facing.

The quiet sovereignty layer

Spacious, reflective, protected, contextual, and sometimes intentionally inaccessible.

This tension could become one of the platform’s defining design ideas.

Contributors could submit:

Every submission should include choices concerning:

17. Community Agreements

The platform should reject:

racism;

colonial appropriation;

misogyny;

homophobia;

transphobia;

ableism;

coercion;

harassment;

stalking;

threats;

non-consensual disclosure;

and punitive pile-ons.

It should also resist subtler patterns:

using radical language to avoid accountability;

treating polyamory as inherently more evolved than monogamy;

treating family estrangement as universally liberating;

treating Indigenous thought as spiritual content available for extraction;

demanding emotional education from marginalized participants;

and using public confession as a substitute for repair.

Moderation principles

Moderation should prioritize:

safety;

context;

education where appropriate;

clear boundaries;

proportional responses;

survivor autonomy;

and prevention of recurring harm.

The site should not promise a perfectly non-carceral community while lacking the capacity to address serious danger.

18. Safety and Crisis Design

Relationship exercises can be dangerous when coercive control or violence is present.

The site should display safety notices beside tools involving:

confrontation;

boundary setting;

leaving;

financial disclosure;

sexual agreements;

digital privacy;

and relationship repair.

The site should explain:

Joint communication tools are not appropriate when one participant is violent, coercive, retaliatory, or controlling.

Visitors should be directed toward localized professional and community resources where necessary.

The platform should not imply that communication can resolve every form of abuse.

19. Accessibility

Relational sovereignty should shape the site’s technical design.

Essential features include:

plain-language summaries;

audio versions;

transcripts;

captions;

screen-reader compatibility;

keyboard navigation;

adjustable text size;

strong contrast;

reduced-motion settings;

printable versions;

low-bandwidth pages;

mobile-friendly tools;

translation;

Accessibility should be treated as a relational obligation, not a technical compliance exercise.

22. Editorial Questions for Every Publication

Before publishing an item, editors should ask:

Does this clarify or merely aestheticize decolonization?

Does it acknowledge material and political conditions?

Does it treat autonomy and accountability together?

Does it recognize power differences?

Does it universalize a culturally specific teaching?

Does it reproduce Indigenous knowledge without context or permission?

Does it treat one relationship model as inherently superior?

Does it account for disability, class, race, gender, sexuality, and citizenship?

Could this advice endanger someone in a coercive relationship?

What practical action becomes possible after reading it?

23. Governance of the Platform

A site about relational sovereignty should not be governed through invisible authority.

Its governance structure should include:

a public editorial charter;

transparent contributor guidelines;

a community advisory group;

paid Indigenous review and consultation;

disability-access review;

conflict-of-interest disclosure;

consent and withdrawal processes;

moderation appeals;

version histories;

and periodic public reviews.

The platform should identify:

who makes decisions;

who receives payment;

who owns the archive;

who controls user data;

who can remove material;

and what happens if the project closes.

The site should minimize data collection and avoid surveillance-based advertising.

26. Measures of Success

Success should not be measured only through traffic or engagement.

More appropriate indicators include:

number of tools completed;

number of zines downloaded or printed;

accessibility usage;

diversity of relationship pathways explored;

return visits to review tools;

contributor retention;

Indigenous and disability-led materials supported;

community groups using workshop materials;

visitors reporting clearer boundaries or agreements;

and evidence that users are expanding care beyond romantic couplehood.

The platform should not collect intimate relationship data merely because it can.

Anonymous, minimal, voluntary feedback is more consistent with its principles.

27. The Central Design Principle

The website should embody the relationship structure it advocates.

It should be:

open, but not extractive;

participatory, but not coercively transparent;

educational, but not authoritative;

politically serious, but practically useful;

confrontational, but not nihilistic;

accessible, but not reductive;

relational, but respectful of privacy;

and unfinished enough that people can help shape it.

Its purpose would not be to teach people the one correct way to relate.

Its purpose would be to help people ask better questions about power, care, consent, responsibility, belonging, and freedom.

The final invitation could be:

You inherited relationship structures you did not design.You are allowed to examine them.You are allowed to refuse them.You are responsible for what you build in their place.
