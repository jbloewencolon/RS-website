UX / UI optimization audit · relationalsovereignty.com · v0.2

Craft is not the problem. Discoverability is.

This site clears bars most sites never reach — zero automated accessibility violations across nine pages, WCAG-passing contrast throughout, no horizontal overflow at any breakpoint, and copy that is careful about consent in ways almost nothing on the web is. Nearly every finding below is therefore about the same failure mode: content that exists but cannot be found, most seriously on the page where being found matters most.

Section 1

Executive summary

The seven findings that matter most, in the order I would fix them.

CriticalUX-01Resources

On the crisis page, ten of eleven resource categories look like empty headings

Every category is a <details> whose <summary> is styled display:flex. That suppresses the browser's disclosure triangle, so a populated-but-closed section is visually indistinguishable from a heading with nothing under it. Only #crisis-lines ships open. Worse, the page's own "Choose what you need" menu does not fix this: I clicked Coercive control and the target stayed shut.

CriticalUX-03Practise

"Clear everything" destroys twenty minutes of work with no confirmation and no undo

The Consent Domains Map deliberately persists nothing. That principle is right, but it makes an accidental click unrecoverable — and the button sits at the top of the tool, beside "Print this map", where a user reaches before they have entered anything and again after they have entered everything.

High impactUX-04Every page

The mobile header consumes 30% of the screen before a single word of content

Eight nav items wrap to three rows and never collapse: a fixed 247px on every page, at every mobile width. On a 320px phone that is 43% of the viewport.

High impactUX-05Home

The six doors sit below the fold at every breakpoint; the only control above it is a novelty

The first door card begins at 1272px on desktop and 1366px on mobile. The single interactive element a first-time visitor sees inside <main> is another question — which is also the most button-shaped thing on the page, and leads nowhere.

High impactUX-07Learn · Archive

The two longest pages have no contents nav — and the component already exists

Learn runs 22,709px on mobile, Archive 23,900px. Neither offers a way in. Behind the Scenes already ships a nine-link <nav aria-label="Contents">. The pattern is built; it just is not applied where the need is greatest.

High impactUX-06Every page

The current page is signalled by colour alone

No aria-current anywhere on the site. The active link is recoloured — and to two different colours depending on whether the page is light or dark. Colour as the sole cue is a WCAG 1.4.1 failure and gives screen-reader users nothing at all.

MediumUX-12Home

The dispatch's privacy promises are made four separate times on one page

"No tracking / one click to leave / never sold" appears in the door card, the left-column bullets, the form footnote, and the footer's Build column — then again, verbatim, on Contribute. Repetition intended as reassurance starts to read as protesting too much.

What holds up — verified, so you do not spend effort here

  • Zero axe-core violations across all nine pages at WCAG 2.1 A/AA plus best-practice. That is rare.
  • Contrast passes throughout. I recomputed every colour pair by hand: body 12.4:1, nav 5.50:1, the faintest dark-section text 4.63:1. Only the link hover state falls short (UX-16).
  • No horizontal overflow at 320, 375, 768, 1280 or 1440. The only elements past the viewport edge are the intentional skip-link and honeypot.
  • "Twenty domains" is accurate. The slice(0,17) / slice(17) split is a visual grouping of 17 + 3, not a shortfall. Likewise "tool 01 of 09" and "seven more tools" reconcile correctly.
  • The Practise ↔ Resources duplication is deliberate and the page says so — the five safety questions are republished "useful with or without the rest of this page". Not redundancy.
  • Archive's sideways-scrolling Venn is a documented choice, announced in copy before the reader meets it.
  • Tab order is clean — skip link first, then masthead, nav, content, in visual order. The honeypot is correctly aria-hidden and tabindex="-1".

Ordered by severity of what the page contains, not by nav order. Each finding states what I observed, why it costs the user something, what to change, and why the change works.

Resources — the highest-stakes page

This is where someone in danger lands. It carries the two most serious findings on the site.

CriticalUX-01Resources

Collapsed crisis sections give no sign they can be opened, and the jump menu does not open them

categories11
shipped open1
marker renderednone
after jump-link clickopen: false
Observed
Each category is <details> with an <h2> inside a <summary> styled display:flex. A flex container generates no ::marker, so the disclosure triangle never paints — computed list-style-type is disclosure-closed, but nothing is drawn. Only #crisis-lines has open. I then clicked Coercive control in the "Choose what you need" menu: the hash changed, the page scrolled, and the section stayed shut. Loading Resources.dc.html#coercive-control directly behaves the same — a browser only auto-expands a <details> when the target is inside it, not when the target is it.
Impact
A distressed user clicks the one link matching their situation and arrives at a screen of nine consecutive headings with nothing beneath any of them. The reasonable inference is that the site has not written this content yet — an inference the page actively encourages, because it says elsewhere that the region-specific section "is not built yet". Real, verified, phone-number-bearing help is one click away and completely invisible.
Change
Three parts. (a) Ship every category open by default on this page — it is a reference list, not an interface to tidy. (b) If collapse is kept, replace display:flex on summary with display:block plus an explicit rotating chevron, or keep flex and add the chevron as a real child element. (c) Point the jump menu at anchors inside each <details>, or add the four-line handler that sets open = true on the fragment target at load and on hashchange.
Why it works
All three restore the page's own promise. Open-by-default is the strongest: it makes the resource list scannable, printable, and searchable with the browser's own find — which is how someone actually looks for a number under stress — and removes the interaction entirely rather than making it more discoverable.
CriticalUX-02Resources

"Not yet built" sections are indistinguishable from populated ones

populated, closed9
genuinely emptyMutual aid networks
visual differenceright-aligned label only
Observed
"Mutual aid networks" carries the right-aligned note Not yet built. "Intimate partner violence and coercive control" carries Canada, national directory. Collapsed, the two rows are typographically identical — same heading treatment, same rule, same absence of content. The only difference is 11px monospace text at the far right edge, 900px away from the heading on desktop.
Impact
The page mixes "there is help here, hidden" with "there is no help here yet" using the same visual form. A user who opens nothing learns the wrong thing about both. This directly undercuts the honesty the site is built on — the roadmap elsewhere is scrupulous about naming what does not exist.
Change
Give the two states different form, not just different words. An empty category should not be a <details> at all: render it as a plain dimmed row with the status inline beside the heading rather than at the opposite margin. Populated categories get the entry count in the same position — 3 organisations — so the label always answers "is there anything in here".
Why it works
State encoded in form survives scanning, low vision, and panic; state encoded only in distant small text does not. A count also pre-answers the question the disclosure was hiding, so the user decides whether to open with information rather than faith.
MediumUX-16Every page

Hovering a link lowers its contrast below AA

rest #2B4C9B6.38:1
hover #3F7A4E4.05:1
AA needs4.50:1
Observed
a:hover{color:#3F7A4E} on the #E7E5DC ground measures 4.05:1 — a regression from the resting blue, and below AA for body-size text. Axe does not catch it because it does not evaluate hover states.
Impact
Interaction makes the target harder to read, which is backwards, and is felt most by exactly the low-vision users the rest of the site accommodates well.
Change
Darken the hover green to roughly #2C5A38 — already in the palette, used for selected interest chips — which measures about 6.5:1. No new colour is introduced.
Why it works
Keeps the green hover language and its meaning while making hover a contrast increase, reinforcing the affordance instead of weakening it.

Practise — the Consent Domains Map

The deepest engagement on the site: a two-step gate, then 20 domains × 5 options = 100 radios, 7,814px tall on desktop.

CriticalUX-03Practise

A destructive action with no confirmation, no undo, and no persistence to fall back on

stated fill time~20 min
confirm stepnone
undonone
autosavenone by design
Observed
reset clears who, picks and notes in a single setState. "Clear everything" is rendered beside "Print this map" at the top of the tool, above all 20 domains, and both are reachable at any point. Because state deliberately never touches storage, there is nothing to recover from.
Impact
One mis-tap ends a twenty-minute session about the most sensitive material on the site, with no path back. The no-persistence rule is a genuine privacy virtue, but it converts an ordinary UI slip into total loss — and the two buttons sit adjacent, so the slip is a near-neighbour of the most common intentional action.
Change
Make the button a two-step control in place: first press swaps its label to a confirm state that must be pressed again, reverting after a few seconds or on any other interaction. Move it away from "Print this map" — bottom of the tool, after the domains, is the natural home for a reset. Keep "Save to a file" adjacent to the top actions where it is genuinely useful before a long session.
Why it works
In-place confirmation adds one press for the deliberate user and blocks the accidental one, without a modal — which would sit badly on a site with no other dialogs. Separating destructive from constructive actions removes the mis-tap at its source rather than catching it afterwards.
MediumUX-17Practise

Every domain shows a condition field before there is any condition to state, and nothing reports progress

domains20
radios100
always-open text fields20
form height7,814px
Observed
"Condition, in your own words" renders under every domain from the outset, including domains marked nothing yet and domains marked yes, freely, where a condition is meaningless. Per-row state reads marked: nothing yet, but there is no aggregate — nothing says how many of the twenty are done.
Impact
The form presents its maximum length at first sight, which is where abandonment decisions get made, and it presents fields that will stay empty for most rows. With no progress signal on a stated twenty-minute task, the user cannot tell whether they are a quarter through or nearly finished — and since nothing is saved, an abandonment is a total loss.
Change
Reveal the condition field when yes, with conditions or not yet — ask me is chosen, and keep it visible once it holds text so nothing is ever hidden after being written. Add a single count near the top actions — 8 of 20 marked — and mirror it in the print output.
Why it works
Progressive disclosure cuts the visible form substantially without removing anything, and ties the field to the two answers that actually invite a condition. A count converts an unbounded scroll into a finite task, which is the single strongest lever on completion — and it doubles as the cue to use "Save to a file" before stopping.
MediumUX-18Practise

Two tools share one page, but only one is announced

Observed
The eyebrow reads "tool 01 of 09 · about 20 minutes to fill in" and the intro describes the Consent Domains Map. Further down sits a second, independent tool — "An ending, prepared for" — with its own gate and its own "Open this tool" button, which is present in the DOM throughout the first tool's gate sequence.
Impact
A user who completes the map and stops has no reason to know a second tool exists; the page's own framing told them there was one. The stray "Open this tool" button during the first tool's gate is also a competing call to action at the moment the flow is asking for a single decision.
Change
Add both tools to the eyebrow's count and give the page a two-item contents list under the intro. Keep the endings tool's trigger, but let it sit inside its own section heading rather than floating as a bare button while another flow is active.
Why it works
Sets the correct expectation at the point of entry, so the second tool is discovered by design rather than by scrolling past the end of the first. No copy changes — only where the existing labels sit.

High impactUX-05Home

Every route into the site is below the fold; the only control above it leads nowhere

first door top · 12801,272px
first door top · 3751,366px

Observed
The hero runs headline, standfirst, and a rotating question with an another question button. The six-door grid — the actual navigation into the site — starts at 1,272px on a 900px-tall desktop viewport, roughly 1.4 screens down. The rotating-question button is bordered, uppercase, 44px tall, and is the most button-like element on the page.
Impact
A first-time visitor gets the thesis quickly, which the hero does well, and then has nothing to act on. The one thing that looks actionable cycles a string in place. Visual prominence and usefulness are inverted at the exact moment a visitor decides whether this site goes anywhere.
Change
Lift the six-door grid into the first screen: reduce the hero's clamp(3rem,7vw,5.5rem) vertical padding, and place the rotating question beside the standfirst on wide viewports rather than beneath it — the hero currently uses only the left 42ch of a 1120px container, so the room is already there. Restyle another question as a quiet text control so its weight matches its function.
Why it works
Puts at least the first row of doors in view without cutting anything, using space that is currently empty. Downgrading the rotating button removes the false affordance and leaves the door cards as the strongest interactive signal — which matches what they are.
MediumUX-12Home · Contribute

The same privacy promise is made four times on Home and twice more on Contribute

Observed
Sentence-level comparison across the rendered pages found the dispatch assurances stated in four places on Home — the door card, the left-column bullets, the form footnote, the footer's Build column — and repeated verbatim on Contribute, including the transactional-email sentence and "Any characters, any script, no validator." The five interest checkboxes are also identical across both forms.
"No tracking pixels. No third parties. Your address is confirmed by email before anything is stored, and one link in every letter removes you."
Impact
Each statement is good, and the first is persuasive. By the fourth, the effect inverts: dense reassurance clustered around a single email field reads as anxiety about the ask. It also inflates the dispatch section — the left column runs long enough that the submit button ends up level with empty space, leaving the word "offer." orphaned at the top of a screen.
Change
Keep one full statement and demote the rest without deleting any. The four left-column bullets are the canonical version — leave them. Drop the form footnote, which repeats two of them within 400px, and let the door card's summary stand alone. No sentence is rewritten; three are removed from a page that already carries them.
Why it works
One clear promise at full strength is more credible than four at diminishing returns, and shortening the right column brings the two dispatch columns closer to equal height, which fixes the orphaned trailing text as a side effect.
MediumUX-13Home · Contribute

Two dispatch forms post to the same list, and nothing tells the visitor that

forms2
endpointidentical
interest optionsidentical ×5
stated differencenone
Observed
Home's dispatch block and Contribute's form both collect email, name, and the same five interests, and both post to the same subscribe worker. Contribute frames its version as "join the dispatch, leave a note, or both"; Home's is signup-only. Nothing on either page acknowledges the other.
Impact
A visitor who subscribed on Home meets what looks like a second, unrelated signup on Contribute and cannot tell whether joining again is required, harmful, or a duplicate. On a site whose entire argument is about knowing exactly what you are consenting to, an ambiguous second ask is the wrong kind of friction.
Change
Make them visibly one thing. Extract a single dispatch component with an optional note field, so Contribute's version reads as the same form with one addition rather than a separate one — and add a line of existing copy on Contribute acknowledging the shared list.
Why it works
Removes the ambiguity at the point of decision and eliminates the maintenance risk of two hand-maintained copies of the same assurances drifting apart — which is also how UX-12's fourfold repetition arose.
MediumUX-09Home · Contribute

A form error names the problem but never marks the field

required attrabsent
aria-invalid on errornever set
field border on errorunchanged
focus after errorstays on button
Observed
Submitting empty produces a role="alert" message — correctly announced — but the email input keeps its resting #C9C6BA border, never receives aria-invalid, and does not receive focus. The message renders after the submit button, below the field it refers to.
Impact
Sighted users read an error at the bottom of the form and must infer which of three inputs it means, while the offending field looks entirely normal. Keyboard and screen-reader users hear the message, then have to navigate backwards past the button and the checkbox group to reach a field that is not marked as the problem.
Change
On failure set aria-invalid="true" on the email input, switch its border to the error red already in the palette (#8B3A2F), move focus to it, and render the message directly beneath the field rather than after the button. Keep role="alert" as is.
Why it works
Binds the error to its cause in all three channels — visual, programmatic, and focus — so every user is left on the field they need to fix. It is the one meaningful gap in an otherwise careful form.
MediumUX-14Home · Contribute

Field hints are absorbed into the field's accessible name

Observed
The hint spans sit inside the <label>, so the ARIA snapshot reports the accessible name as textbox "Name or pseudonym Optional. Any characters, any script, no validator. We do not check."
Impact
A screen-reader user hears the entire hint every time focus enters the field, and again on any re-announcement. Voice-control users must speak an unusable name to target it. Axe passes this because a label does exist.
Change
Give each hint an id, move it outside the <label>, and reference it with aria-describedby. Same for the "Optional. Choose none, one, or all." paragraph under the fieldset legend.
Why it works
The name becomes "Name or pseudonym" and the hint is still read — as a description, after a pause, and only when useful. Nothing is hidden and nothing is reworded.
MediumUX-10Home

Four-item grids wrap to three columns at every desktop width

content box1,024px

Observed
The roadmap and the footer both use repeat(auto-fit,minmax(240px,1fr)) with a 2.4rem gap. Inside a 1120px container with 48px padding the usable width is 1,024px, which fits three tracks, not four. Because max-width caps the container, this never resolves — 1440px behaves exactly like 1280px.
Impact
The roadmap's four stages read as a progression — Open now → In progress → Next → Someday, or never — and the wrap drops the fourth onto a second row directly beneath the first, where it reads as a continuation of "Open now" rather than the end of the sequence. The footer orphans "Reuse" the same way.
Change
Lower the track minimum to about 200px, or set repeat(4,1fr) above 1100px with the auto-fit rule as the fallback below it.
Why it works
Four columns restore left-to-right reading of a sequence that is genuinely ordered, so the layout encodes the roadmap's meaning instead of fighting it. 240 → 200 is enough; the narrowest column holds "Someday, or never" comfortably.
MediumUX-15Home

Resources is the only major page with no presence on the homepage

Observed
The six doors are Manifesto, Learn, Practise, Archive, Contribute, and the dispatch anchor. Invitation is linked from the paragraph above them. Resources appears only in the header nav and the footer list — and the dispatch's own interest checkbox includes "I'm looking for support and resources", so the need is acknowledged without being routed anywhere.

The page a person in difficulty most needs is the hardest to reach from the entry point, and is buried seventh in an eight-item nav that on mobile has already wrapped to a third row.

PolishUX-20Home · Learn · Practise · Invitation

Global chrome — header, nav, footer

Identical markup on all nine pages, so each finding here multiplies by nine.

High impactUX-04Every page

The header never collapses, and takes 30–43% of a mobile screen

header height247px
of 375×81230%

Observed
Eight nav links, each min-height:44px plus .4rem padding, in a wrapping flex row. At 375px they occupy three rows; the header measures a constant 247px at both 375 and 320. There is no menu toggle at any width.
Impact
On every mobile page load the first thing a reader sees is a three-row block of uppercase monospace links, and the page's own headline is pushed to 372px. The 44px targets are the right instinct — they are what makes the block tall — so the fix has to preserve them rather than shrink them.
Change
Below roughly 700px, collapse the nav behind a single 44px control using <details> so it needs no JavaScript, keeping the wordmark and toggle on one row. That returns roughly 190px — most of a fold — on every page. The eight items stay exactly as they are once opened.
Why it works
Trades a permanent cost on every page for one tap by the minority of visitors who navigate laterally, and gives the content the top of the screen back. A <details> toggle also matches the disclosure pattern the site already uses — provided it ships with the visible chevron that UX-01 asks for.
High impactUX-06Every page

No aria-current, and the active-page colour changes between pages

aria-current site-wide0
active · light pages#366943
active · dark pages#DB9E2A
non-colour cuenone
Observed
The current page's nav link is recoloured — green on light pages, amber on the Manifesto's dark ground — with no weight change, no underline, and no aria-current attribute anywhere in the codebase. Both colours pass contrast; that is not the issue.
Impact
Colour is the sole means of conveying "you are here", which fails WCAG 1.4.1 and disappears entirely for colour-blind users, in high-contrast modes, and in print. Screen-reader users get no signal at all. And because the token differs by page theme, the cue has to be relearned rather than recognised.
Change
Add aria-current="page" to the active link and pair the colour with a non-colour cue — a 2px underline offset below the link, or a bumped weight. Use one semantic token for "current" and let it resolve per theme, so the meaning is constant even where the hue is not.
Why it works
Three redundant signals — colour, shape, and semantics — mean the state survives every rendering context. It is an attribute and a border rule on markup that already exists on all nine pages.

Observed
The same min-height:44px plus padding is applied to the footer's vertical link list, so eight links stack to roughly 390px with visibly uneven gaps. With four stacked columns the footer reaches 931px — more than a full screen of chrome after the content ends.

A reader who finishes a page scrolls through more than a screen of navigation to reach the end. The generous gaps also make the link list read as loosely spaced rather than deliberately grouped.

PolishUX-21Archive

The Venn diagram scrolls sideways with no visual sign that it can

Observed
The "Held in common" diagram is a min-width:700px grid in an overflow-x:auto container. At 375px a reader sees a little over half of it. The page announces this in copy beforehand — "on a narrow screen this diagram scrolls sideways" — which is more than most sites do.
Impact
The announcement only works for someone reading sequentially. A scanner meets a cut-off diagram with no edge treatment and no scrollbar until they touch it.
Change
Add a soft fade or hairline shadow on the right edge of the scroll container while more content remains.
Why it works
Gives the scanner the same information the sentence gives the reader, at the moment and place they need it.

Learn and Archive — the long reads

High impactUX-07Learn · Archive

The site's two longest pages are the only long pages with no contents nav

Learn · mobile22,709px
Archive · mobile23,900px
Learn headings25 h2 · 17 h3
contents navabsent on both
Observed
Behind the Scenes opens with a proper <nav aria-label="Contents"> listing nine in-page anchors. It is the only page on the site that has one. Learn (13 principles) and Archive (60 headings) have no in-page anchors at all beyond the skip link — on mobile they run to about 28 and 29 screens respectively.
Impact
A reader who wants principle 09, or who followed a reference to a specific text, can only scroll. There is no way to see the shape of the page, no way to resume where they left off, and nothing to link to when sending someone a specific principle. This is the plainest gap between the site's own best pattern and its application.
Change
Reuse the Behind the Scenes contents component verbatim on Learn and Archive, with stable anchor ids per principle and per shelf section. Both pages are Hugo-generated from data/principles.yaml and data/archive.yaml, so the list can be built from the same data as the content and cannot drift out of sync.
Why it works
No new design language, no new component, no copy written — an existing pattern applied where the need is largest. Stable ids also make individual principles and texts citable, which suits a site built to be forked and passed on.
High impactUX-08Archive

Observed
The section headed "The fastest honest route in" lists numbered texts — 01 Wildcat — Replacing Exclusive Sovereignty, 02 Kuokkanen, 03 TallBear, and so on. Every item is plain text. Not one links to the corresponding shelf entry above it, on a page with no contents nav and 60 headings.

Section 4

Quick wins

Ordered by impact per unit of effort. The first three are, between them, perhaps twenty lines of CSS and markup, and they resolve two of the three critical findings.

  1. Ship every Resources category open. One attribute per <details>. Immediately makes the crisis page's real content visible and searchable with the browser's own find. Resolves UX-01's worst effect on its own.
  2. Give summary a visible chevron site-wide. Change display:flex to display:block with an explicit marker, or add a chevron child. Fixes all 16 markerless disclosures across Resources, Practise, and Behind the Scenes in one rule.
  3. Add aria-current="page" plus an underline on the active nav link. One attribute and one border rule, applied to markup that is already identical on nine pages.
  4. Lower the two grid minimums from 240px to 200px. Two characters each. Restores the roadmap's four-stage progression and un-orphans the footer's "Reuse" column.
  5. Darken the link hover to #2C5A38. A colour already in the palette. Turns a contrast regression into a contrast improvement.
  6. Set the footer nav to two columns on mobile. Recovers about 195px on every page with no change to any tap target.
  7. Move the three form hints to aria-describedby. Shortens two accessible names from a full sentence to their actual labels.
  8. Make "Clear everything" confirm in place. A boolean and a label swap; closes the only unrecoverable action on the site.
  9. Link the Archive reading order to its shelf entries. Anchor tags around text that already matches the headings it should point to.
  10. Delete the dispatch form's footnote. It repeats two bullets sitting 400px to its left. Nothing is rewritten and nothing is lost.
Section 5

Strategic opportunities

Larger moves worth planning rather than patching.

Decide what disclosure means on this site, once

Sixteen <details> elements are doing three unrelated jobs: hiding optional depth on Practise, tidying a long reference list on Resources, and holding supplementary notes on Behind the Scenes. They share one visual treatment and none of them signals it can be opened. The underlying question is not styling — it is whether collapse is appropriate for reference content at all.

A single rule would settle it: collapse things a reader may want to skip; never collapse things a reader may need to find. Under that rule Resources opens by default, Practise's optional grounding exercises stay collapsed, and every remaining disclosure gets one consistent, visibly openable treatment. This turns three critical and high findings into one design decision.

Build a real navigation layer for long-form reading

Four pages now exceed 11,000px on desktop and three exceed 22,000px on mobile, and the roadmap adds more tools and a stories section. Behind the Scenes' contents nav is the right seed, but a page of Archive's size needs more: a contents list, stable per-section ids, and — given that Learn, Archive, and Behind the Scenes are all Hugo-generated from YAML — a shared partial that derives the list from the same data as the content.

The payoff compounds. Every principle and every archive text becomes individually linkable, which matters disproportionately for a site whose stated measure of success is being forked, printed, and passed on. Right now nobody can send a friend principle 09.

Give the homepage a first screen that shows the shape of the site

The hero currently spends a full viewport on the thesis and puts every route below the fold. The thesis is strong and should stay — but the six doors are the best thing on the page, and no visitor sees them without scrolling past a control that does nothing.

Recomposing the hero to use its empty right half, and lifting the first row of doors into view, would let a visitor grasp both the idea and the map in one screen. This is the highest-leverage conversion change available that does not touch a word of copy.

Resolve the two dispatch forms into one relationship

Home and Contribute both post to the same subscribe endpoint with identical interest checkboxes and identical assurance copy, but Contribute's is framed as "join the dispatch, leave a note, or both" while Home's is dispatch-only. Nothing tells a visitor who has seen one what the other adds, and someone who signs up on Home has no idea Contribute's form is the same list.

Worth deciding deliberately: either make Home's the sole signup and let Contribute link to it, or make the difference explicit in the layout — a shared component with one optional extra field. Either removes a duplicated form, a duplicated set of promises, and the maintenance risk of the two drifting apart.

Treat the Consent Domains Map as a session, not a page

It is a twenty-minute, hundred-control task with no autosave — correctly, for privacy — reached through a two-step gate and sharing a page with a second tool. The pieces of a good session are already there: the gate, the file export, the print view, per-row state. What is missing is the connective tissue: a completion count, a visible save prompt before the user leaves, progressive disclosure of the condition fields, and a confirm on the one action that destroys everything.

None of these compromise the no-storage principle. Together they would substantially raise the number of maps that reach a printer — which is the roadmap's own stated measure of success.
