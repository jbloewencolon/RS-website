Learn — the idea, in plain language · about 6 minutes

Freedom through relationship, not from it.

Relational sovereignty asks what would change if relationships were understood as living systems of consent, responsibility, reciprocity, and self-determination rather than structures of possession.

It is not a finished doctrine, and this page does not present it as one. Thirteen principles follow. Each carries a question you can actually ask of a relationship you are in.

  1. 01

    No ownership

    People do not become property through romance, marriage, sexuality, family, caregiving, or shared history.

    What access am I treating as an entitlement?

  2. 02

    Self-determination

    Each person should retain meaningful authority over their body, identity, beliefs, associations, and life direction.

    Which decisions belong primarily to me, and which genuinely require shared governance?

  3. 03

    Interdependence

    Needing people does not eliminate sovereignty. The problem is not dependence but dependence controlled by someone else. And some dependence is not chosen and cannot be left — guardianship, congregate care, a body that needs another body to get through the day. Sovereignty there is not exit. It is who else is in the room, what can be contested, and whether anyone outside would know.

    Do I have meaningful influence over the conditions under which I receive or provide care?

  4. 04

    Consent

    Consent must be specific, informed, ongoing, and materially possible. A label is not blanket permission.

    What would happen if someone said no?

  5. 05

    Non-domination

    Power should not be arbitrary, hidden, or impossible to challenge. Difference is real; domination is not inevitable.

    Who controls the resources, information, recognition, or access in this relationship?

  6. 06

    Relational accountability

    Freedom does not remove responsibility for foreseeable effects on others.

    Who bears the cost of my choices?

  7. 07

    Refusal

    A relationship should contain space for disagreement, boundaries, withdrawal, and change.

    Can refusal occur without retaliation?

  8. 08

    Opacity

    Intimacy does not require complete access or total legibility. But opacity is a right against people who want more of you than they are owed — it is not available against institutions that compel disclosure to release care, money, status, or documents. Refusing to be read is a privilege unevenly distributed, and the framework should not mistake one for the other.

    What parts of another person am I assuming I have a right to know? And where is this person already required to be legible to someone with power over them?

  9. 09

    Reciprocity

    Relationships involve giving and receiving, but reciprocity does not require identical contributions.

    Are care, effort, risk, and benefit distributed in a way people experience as fair?

  10. 10

    Repair

    Accountability requires more than apology. Repair does not guarantee reconciliation.

    What material or behavioural change would repair require?

  11. 11

    Transformation

    Relationships may change form without becoming meaningless.

    Are we preserving the relationship, or merely preserving its current category?

  12. 12

    Relational futurity

    The purpose is not simply to reject harmful relationships, but to create forms of connection in which people and communities can flourish.

    What kind of relational world are our present practices creating?

  13. 13

    The carer is inside the relationship

    The person providing care is a party to the relation, not staff administering it. Their refusal is constrained by someone else's survival, which is a real constraint and not a moral failing. A framework that protects only the cared-for has built another caste.

    Who relieves this person, and what happens if no one does?

A clause, not a principle — 06 and 07 without a tiebreaker

Continuity of care

Leaving is not abandonment. But when someone depends on you for survival — food, medication, housing, personal care, money, immigration status — your leaving moves that need somewhere else. It does not dissolve it, and the person holding it next is usually already tired.

So if you can go safely: before you go, name who now holds what you were holding, and check that they know they are holding it. If nobody does, say that out loud to someone who is not the person you are leaving.

If going safely means going suddenly, go suddenly. Nothing in this framework is a reason to stay. This is written against people who use the word sovereignty to walk away from a dependant — and it is not available to anyone who wants to use it to keep you.

Who is holding what I was holding, and do they know it?

Principle 06 (relational accountability) and principle 07 (refusal) can pull against each other with nothing here to say which wins. This clause is the tiebreaker for one specific case — dependency, at the moment of leaving — not a general answer. If you are not sure whether leaving is safe, Resources has somewhere to start.

One word, four jobs

Four things this word is doing.

Political sovereignty.

Jurisdiction over territory and people. Indigenous nationhood. This site does not touch it and cannot advance it.

Jurisdictional sovereignty.

Whose law governs a body of data, on whose territory the machine sits, who holds deletion authority. This is the sense in which a website can have anything to do with sovereignty at all.

Relational sovereignty.

Authority over the terms of one's own relations — the sense borrowed from Matthew Wildcat and, independently, from work on assistive technology. This is the site's operative sense.

The rhetorical sense.

Not being owned. This is the manifesto's register and it is the loosest of the four.

The first and the third are not the same kind of thing. Where this site is persuasive partly because they rhyme, that is worth naming rather than using.

And an objection this site has not answered

Taiaiake Alfred argues sovereignty is inseparable from the frameworks of colonisation, and that Indigenous governance is better served by concepts that are not borrowed from the people who used this one to negate it. That argument is on the shelf. This site keeps the word because Wildcat rebuilt it and because the jurisdictional question — whose law governs here, who holds deletion authority — is the right question for intimacy and "autonomy" cannot ask it. That is a reason, not a refutation. If the paid review says the word does more harm than work, the word goes and this paragraph becomes the record of why it stayed this long.

Principle 08, applied to this website

Total legibility is a form of domination.

Opacity in a principle is easy. Opacity in software is a governance decision, so here is what it actually amounts to on this site — the parts that are real, and the parts that are not built.

real, one line of config

The crawler is refused, selectively

The workshop and the contribution pages are disallowed in robots.txt. Discovery by a third party is not a condition of existing, and a publisher can decline the register.

real, by having nothing

There is no consent banner

Not because consent was waived, but because nothing is tracked, no cookie is set, and nothing is stored. The strongest refusal of consent theatre is having nothing to recite at you.

real, as absence

Some things are simply not here

No intimate data is collected because it is technically possible. The tools keep their answers in your browser. An archive record can exist while its content does not — absence is not incompleteness.

not built

Community-set access rules

Protocol-gated material, where a community rather than this project decides who may see what, is unimplemented. Until it is, nothing that genuinely requires protocol is held here.

⌖ The caveat that governs all of it: opacity is owed to the crawler and the market, never to the disabled reader. Nothing here is hidden from assistive technology and no semantic structure is stripped to look less legible. Withhold from extraction; disclose fully to those who need access to participate. Getting this backwards produces a site that is self-congratulatory and practically exclusionary.

Protocol-gated access — where a community, not this project, decides who may see what and under which relationship — already exists in Mukurtu CMS, the Local Contexts TK and BC Labels, and the CARE Principles. It is not implemented here. The fault list says so.

What's planned, not what's built

A conceptual map is planned. This page is what's actually written.

Nineteen further topics are named but not one is linked, because an unwritten page marked drafted would be the small lie this site is trying not to tell. The full list, and why nothing on it is dated, lives on the roadmap.

Take it further

Read the manifesto for the refusal these principles come from. Work through the Consent Domains Map to apply them. Then leave for the writers themselves — this page is a summary, and a summary is not the thought.

A caution held throughout

The relational is not virtuous simply by being relational. Kinship, community, and tradition contain their own hierarchies, and a design that signals warmth while leaving power unmapped has produced decoration, not sovereignty.
